History before literature, or literature before history?

For 21 countries, the year when the Library of Congress history class and the literature class for the same country each reached half of their records. Source: Library of Congress MARC distribution, 2016.

1. The midpoints, country by country

Each cumulative curve (records published through 2011) is divided by its total and fitted with a logistic curve. The inflection year t0 is the year when about half of the records had been published. A country’s history class and its literature class are joined by a line. Countries are sorted from those where history came first (top) to those where literature came first (bottom). The shaded band is the middle half of all 221 subclasses with a good fit, and the dashed line is their median (1985).

What the chart shows
  • In England and Scotland the history class reached its midpoint about ten years before the literature class. France, the Netherlands and Ireland show a smaller lead for history (4–6 years).
  • For Germany, Belgium, Italy, Spain, Portugal and Sweden the two midpoints are within about 1.5 years of each other.
  • In Denmark, Norway and Iceland the literature class is 3–5 years ahead.
  • In Poland, Russia, the Czech lands, Ukraine, Belarus, Lithuania and Latvia the literature class is 7–14 years ahead of the history class, and the histories reach their midpoint in the 1990s.

2. The gradient on a map

The same gap, drawn on the countries. Orange countries are those where the history class reached its midpoint first, blue where the literature class did. The colour deepens with the size of the gap (up to 14 years). Grey countries have no pair in this comparison.

Hatched: Crimea, Donetsk and Luhansk, Ukrainian territory unlawfully occupied by Russia. Czech lands: Czechia is coloured, and Slovakia’s history is in the same class. Wales and Northern Ireland are not coloured (Wales had too few literature records; Northern Ireland has no separate class).

West to east

Each country’s gap against the longitude of its centre. The trend runs from history-first in the west to literature-first in the east (rank correlation 0.68 across the 21 countries, 0.77 without Iceland). It is mostly a history effect: the history midpoint moves later toward the east (rank correlation 0.58) much more than the literature midpoint does (0.27).

Longitude is the rough centre of each country, so it is only a proxy for position. Twenty-one countries are not independent observations (Ireland and Britain, or the Baltic states, share histories), so the correlations describe the pattern here, not a general rule.

5. How to read this, and its limits

  • Publication year, not acquisition year. The curves show when material was published and cataloged, not when the Library acquired it.
  • Mixed literature classes. PQ Spanish includes Latin America, PQ Portuguese includes Brazil, and PT German includes Austria and Switzerland.
  • Ireland and Scotland. The Library classes major Irish and Scottish authors in the English by-period tables, so the Irish and Scottish literature curves here use only the regional sections (PR8700–8893 and PR8500–8693). They cover a small part of that writing, and the Scotland and Ireland comparisons are weaker than the others. Wales and Luxembourg had too few records to fit.
  • Mature classes. England, Scotland and English literature have 10–90% rise times of about 90 to 140 years, so their midpoints are rough. The order is solid, but exact years are not.
  • Years after 2011 are incomplete in the 2016 file, which is why the fits stop at 2011.
  • Why these gaps exist is not shown by these data. Differences in publishing, acquisition programs or cataloging practice could each contribute.

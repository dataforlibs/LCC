When did each country's publishing peak?

Records in the Library of Congress catalog grouped by place of publication (the country code in the MARC 008 field). For each place a logistic curve is fitted to the cumulative share of records by publication year; its derivative is a bell-shaped peak, and the peak year is the inflection year of the sigmoid. Source: Library of Congress MARC distribution, 2016.

1. Peak year on the map

Each country is coloured by the year its fitted peak falls, from earlier (light) to later (dark). Hover a country to see its curves, or click to keep the popup open. Countries with fewer than 300 records with a publication year up to 2011 are left grey. Czechoslovakia, Yugoslavia and East Germany no longer exist and their records are filed under their own codes, so they are drawn in the insets below the map on today’s borders.

Records of Czechoslovakia, Yugoslavia and East Germany stop when the states ended, so their curves are cut short and are not added to the successor states on the map. The Soviet Union has no inset: almost all Soviet-period records carry the code of a republic (Russia, Ukraine, Belarus and so on), so they are already counted in those present-day countries, and the Soviet code itself holds only 129 records.

2. The peaks

Each row is one place. The shaded curve is the derivative of the fitted sigmoid, so it shows how fast the cumulative share grew in each year; the dot marks the peak. Rows are sorted by peak year. In shape view every curve is scaled to the same height so the timing can be compared; in volume view the height shows records per year on a common scale, so large publishers stand out.

4. What this does not show

  • Place of publication is the country of the publisher recorded in the catalog record, not the nationality of the author or the language of the book.
  • The curves describe the publications the Library holds, which depend on its acquisition policy, so a peak is the peak of what reached the catalog, not of a country's total output. The United States and the United Kingdom are collected most completely.
  • The distribution is incomplete after about 2011, so fits stop there. A peak after 2011 is an extrapolation and should be read with care; the table shows R² and the 10–90% duration for each fit.
  • US states, Canadian provinces and Australian states are added up to their countries. The United Kingdom is shown as one place by default, with England, Scotland, Wales and Northern Ireland available through the checkbox; records coded only “United Kingdom” are not assigned to a nation.
  • A small spike at 1900 in the observed counts comes from records that carry 1900 as a default or approximate date; it has little effect on the fits.
  • Records coded as unknown or several places are left out. Czechoslovakia, Yugoslavia and East Germany are kept as their own entries and are not merged into Czechia, Slovakia, Serbia or Germany, so the Czech, Slovak, Serbian and German curves cover only records coded to the present-day states.
